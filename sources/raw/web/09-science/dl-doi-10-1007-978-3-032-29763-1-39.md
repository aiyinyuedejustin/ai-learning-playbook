Abstract

Acquisition of cognitive skills depends on building problem-solving schemas. These schemas develop most effectively when instructional support aligns with learners’ current level of expertise. Novices benefit from worked-out examples that make the solution rationale explicit, whereas more advanced learners benefit from prompts that elicit self-explanations, allowing them to complete solution steps on their own. Tutors based on Large Language Model (LLM) provide scalable, dialog-based assistance that can deliver such instructional support. However, there is scarce evidence on how personalizing tutoring based on initial skill stage with LLMs affects learning processes.

In this study, we investigate the effects of personalizing instructional support by stage-aware scaffolding in LLM-based tutors on learners’ tutor usage, reasoning behavior, and mental effort. To this end, we developed an LLM-based tutor grounded in the cognitive skill acquisition theory. It uses worked-out examples with scaffolds for novices and fades this support out for more advanced learners. We used a randomized controlled experiment in a master’s-level programming course to compare the stage-aware tutor with a baseline tutor. Learners were randomly assigned to one of the two tutor groups. Results indicate that learners using the skill-stage-aware tutor (vs. baseline) sent more messages and were active across more days, and their interactions were more likely to reflect deep rather than shallow engagement. Furthermore, learners who used the skill-stage-aware tutor invested higher mental effort in their schema construction. The results indicate that theory-grounded personalization to learners’ initial skill stages can provide process-level scaffolding during self-study and shape learner–tutor dialogue toward cognitively productive activity.
