Education has shaped my life for as long as I can remember. I grew up in a family of academics; on my mother’s side I have three generations of teachers, lecturers, and headmistresses behind me, and I inherited a lifelong love of learning for its own sake. For a decade I also tutored mathematics privately, gaining a great deal of insight and enjoyment from teaching students of varying abilities and backgrounds.

Against that backdrop, the unstoppable rise of generative AI fills me with both excitement and apprehension. The ways in which we teach and learn have evolved slowly and unevenly, with great strides punctuated by periods of stagnation or decline. I believe we are at another crucial point of inflection.

Generative AI is already revolutionising how people acquire, process, and use new information in classrooms and beyond, with attitudes ranging from fundamental scepticism to wide-eyed enthusiasm.

**This article examines both sides of the coin and argues that while the short-term promise of AI is real and significant, its longer-term effects on learning, critical thinking, and metacognition may be far more troubling.**

## The Promise: Solving the Two-Sigma Problem

The structural problems of typical classroom education are well known. Large class sizes force teachers to pace their lessons for the middle, leaving struggling students behind and the brightest unchallenged. Overworked and underpaid teachers lack the time to provide detailed personalised feedback, and engagement is an ongoing struggle.

**What is perhaps less widely appreciated is the scale of what one-to-one tuition can achieve. [Benjamin Bloom’s landmark 1984 study](https://web.mit.edu/5.95/readings/bloom-two-sigma.pdf) established the** **two-sigma problem: students receiving individual tutoring performed two standard deviations better than those in conventional instruction, meaning the average tutored student outperformed 98% of their classroom-taught peers.**


The result is striking, but it doesn’t surprise me. I repeatedly saw students who had needed support shoot to the top of their class after I started tutoring them. Crucially, this was less due to anything special I was doing than to the intrinsic power of personalised instruction and attention. But until now, this kind of private tuition has been a luxury, largely inaccessible to those who need it the most.

This is exactly the gap that generative AI promises to fill. Unlike their scripted, rule-based predecessors—the so-called Intelligent Tutoring Systems of earlier decades—GenAI tutors can produce contextually relevant and natural-sounding responses on the fly, deploy the Socratic method to foster independent learning, and make students feel genuinely attended to. LLM frameworks can adapt to different personalities, increasing engagement by meeting psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

Tools like Khan Academy’s Khanmigo are already deployed at scale, and early research results are promising. [Kestin et al. (2025)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6) found that students using a pedagogically informed AI tutor learned significantly more in less time than peers in traditional active-learning classes while reporting higher engagement. In a randomised controlled trial by [Lu et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16820v1), AI-mediated feedback led to higher-quality revised essay drafts than human-only feedback, roughly equivalent to moving a student from the 50th to the 70th percentile.

More generally, recent meta-analyses have found significant positive impacts on learning performance as well as cognitive and emotional engagement. **If these results generalise, AI tutoring could be one of the most democratising developments in education since the printing press.**

## When Easier Isn’t Better: Cognitive Offloading and Desirable Difficulty

Yet when you dig deeper, a more worrying picture begins to emerge. [Bastani et al. (2025)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122) conducted a large-scale study on high school mathematics learning and found that students using a Socratic AI tutor improved their performance on practice problems by up to 127 percent. But when the tutor was removed for an exam, the advantage evaporated entirely, while unfettered access to basic GPT during practice sessions actually lowered student performance by 17 percent compared to the control group. The students using AI noticed none of this. **In fact, they perceived that they had performed significantly better than everyone else.**

The mechanism at the root of this is *cognitive offloading*: the use of external tools to reduce the mental processing requirements of a task. Cognitive offloading, for instance through calculators or search engines, is not new or inherently negative. It helps us overcome memory limitations and achieve cognitive feats that would not otherwise be possible. But it can also come with downsides.

Psychologists distinguish between different types of cognitive load. In particular, *germane cognitive load* refers to the processing involved in building and integrating “schemas”: knowledge structures in long-term memory. Reduce it and you may feel more fluent, but you also remove the mechanism that produces meaningful comprehension and makes learning stick.

[Research on ‘desirable difficulties’](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf) corroborates this: conditions that feel harder in the moment often optimise long-term retention and transfer. The *generation effect* refers to the lasting benefit of generating a result or solution independently rather than being shown it. Any time we look up something we could have successfully strived towards ourselves, we forfeit a powerful learning opportunity. AI makes bypassing this opportunity almost effortless.

In a [study by Barcaui (2025)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590291125010186), undergraduates who used ChatGPT as a study aid scored significantly lower on a surprise retention test 45 days later. [Akgun and Toker (2025)](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/repository/short-term-gains-long-term-gaps-impact-genai-and-search-technologies-retention) found that AI improved performance on lower-level cognitive tasks but reduced it on higher-level ones such as analysing, evaluating, and creating. Three weeks later, even the lower-level gains had vanished. AI may produce short-term improvements, but the bigger picture is considerably less clear.

One solution might be to engineer AI tutors with guardrails that actively enforce desirable difficulty. For instance, a system could withhold solutions until an attempt is made, deliver help through graded hints, demand self-explanation, and log students’ intermediate steps and revisions for teachers to review. Rewarding understanding rather than mere results may help incentivise and preserve some of the cognitive steps on which learning depends.

But there is a deeper issue that design alone may not solve. Learning depends not only on cognitive processes, but on the self-discipline to endure confusion and persist through difficulty. In mathematics, progress often requires sitting with a problem, resisting the urge to abandon it, and returning to it until one’s understanding begins to shift. LLMs make it easy to escape that effort the moment it becomes uncomfortable, weakening one of the main ways in which intellectual perseverance is formed.


## Critical Thinking Under Threat

And self-discipline is not the only cognitive virtue at risk. Critical thinking is the capacity to question, evaluate, synthesise, and reason about information to reach well-founded conclusions. Derived from the Greek *kritikos*, ‘able to judge or discern,’ it allows us to spot bias and misinformation, revise our views in light of evidence, and solve problems creatively. It is foundational to academic success, competent citizenship and professional life.

The evidence that AI undermines critical thinking is accumulating. [In one study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563224002541), undergraduates were randomly assigned to use either a search engine or an LLM to make and justify recommendations on a scientific topic. LLMs reduced mental effort but also decreased reasoning quality, with search engine users listing significantly more relevant arguments.

The effect was entirely mediated by germane cognitive load: web search required students to actively collect and synthesise information from multiple sources, a demanding process that enhanced understanding. LLM users received ready-made coherent, structured answers and, in doing so, bypassed a crucial step.

Similarly, [Gerlich (2025)](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4698/15/1/6) found a strong negative correlation between frequent AI use and critical thinking scores. Notably, younger and less-educated users were most affected; more educated users maintained a sceptical stance, treating AI outputs as fallible and requiring verification. **AI, it seems, may amplify existing cognitive inequalities rather than dissolve them.**

Finally, some of the most concrete findings come from [Kosmyna et al. (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872), who used EEG to measure brain signals while students wrote essays using an LLM, a search engine, or working unaided. Neural activation and connectivity scaled systematically downward with the level of external support: the brain-only group showed the strongest networks; the LLM group showed up to 55% reduced connectivity.

After completing their essays, 83% of the LLM group were unable to provide a single correct quote from their own work, compared with just 11% in the other groups. They had generated content without truly internalising it.

Yet the study also contained a more hopeful result: when the brain-only group was subsequently allowed to use an LLM on a topic they had already worked through independently, neural connectivity increased significantly across all frequency bands. Struggle first, AI second, appears to be a genuinely different cognitive experience—and a better one.

## The Metacognitive Trap

Perhaps the most concerning yet underdiscussed aspect of AI learning is its effect on metacognition: the capacity to accurately reason about and assess one’s own understanding. Metacognitive skills are essential to effective learning, and strong predictors of academic achievement. They help us monitor our understanding, identify gaps, and adjust our approaches and goals accordingly.

The research here repeatedly tells the same story. As in the Bastani study cited earlier, [Lehmann et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047) found that students with access to LLMs perceived they had learned more than they actually had. [Fan et al. (2024)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/386649880_Beware_of_metacognitive_laziness_Effects_of_generative_artificial_intelligence_on_learning_motivation_processes_and_performance) found that while ChatGPT significantly improved essay scores, it did not increase knowledge gain, knowledge transfer, or intrinsic motivation—and crucially, AI users displayed fewer metacognitive processes such as self-evaluation and orientation, a pattern the authors term “metacognitive laziness.”

The aptly titled *“[AI Makes You Smarter but None the Wiser](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563225002262)”* reveals similar outcomes: LLM assistance improved results on logical reasoning questions, but increased participants’ tendency to overestimate their performance. Intriguingly, AI use also levelled out the Dunning-Kruger effect, with more competent participants no better at assessing themselves than less competent ones.

Meanwhile, higher AI literacy was associated with greater confidence—and less accurate self-evaluation. We may be becoming measurably more capable in some narrow sense, and significantly less aware of the limits of our own understanding.

This dynamic extends beyond the classroom and into professional life. In high-stakes fields where problems rapidly evolve, it becomes dangerous. In randomised experiments with professional software developers, [researchers at Anthropic](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245) found that using AI while mastering a new programming library impaired conceptual understanding, code reading, and debugging—the very skills needed to supervise AI systems.

If coupled with diminished metacognitive awareness, this creates a troubling paradox: **the technology that may most urgently require rigorous human oversight could undermine our capacity to provide it.**


## Conclusion

The potential of generative AI in learning remains significant. It can reduce the time teachers spend on administrative and routine tasks, freeing them up for more individualised attention. For students lacking access and resources, it may still prove to be a net positive.

But as we have seen, there is a real danger that AI could erode our most distinctive cognitive abilities without us even realising it. To harness its promise without harming human intelligence and skills in the long-term, systems must be put in place that preserve and promote the very kinds of thinking that have allowed us to invent intelligent machines in the first place.

Kosmyna et al.’s findings point towards a workable principle: **independent effort first, AI help second.** In practice, this may prove a challenge. Pedagogical LLM tutors can provide guardrails and gating, but a combined approach will likely be needed. Pen-and-paper sessions, oral examinations, and assessment designs that resist AI completion all have a role to play.

Ultimately, we should not forget why education and learning exist in the first place and what they are for: transmitting accumulated knowledge across generations; producing critical thinkers, competent workers, and capable citizens; and cultivating the problem-solving skills and creativity that have allowed humans to transform their world.

As AI grows ever more capable, humanity will face challenges it has never seen before. We will need to think, evaluate, judge, and strategise alongside systems that will likely outperform us in most measurable ways. **This is not the time to give up on learning. It is the time to be more ambitious about it than ever.**
